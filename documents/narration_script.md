## Scene 2: What Is a Limit? (0:53 – 1:50)

**[0:53 – 1:01] Notation appears**
This is where limits come in. We write it like this: the limit as x approaches a of f of x equals L.

**[1:01 – 1:11] Three cards (x, a, L)**
It's super simple to read: x is the input number, a is the target number we are moving toward, and L is the value that the function gets closer to.

**[1:11 – 1:23] Number line — left dot moves**
Imagine a number line around 2. From the left side, a point steps closer and closer: 1.5, 1.8, 1.9, 1.99, and 1.999.

**[1:23 – 1:34] Number line — right dot moves**
From the right side, another point moves inward: 2.5, 2.2, 2.1, 2.01, and 2.001.

**[1:34 – 1:40] Both dots near 2**
Both points get closer and closer to 2, without ever needing to actually land on 2.

**[1:40 – 1:50] Bottom takeaway**
That's the whole idea of a limit. It doesn't ask what happens right AT a point—it asks what happens as we get closer and closer to that point.

## Scene 4: Solving It Step by Step (2:44 – 4:03)

**[2:44 – 2:53] Top equation**
Now let's solve this algebraically, step by step: the limit as x approaches 2 of x squared minus 4 over x minus 2.

**[2:53 – 3:04] Step 1: Plugging in 2**
Step 1: Try plugging in x equals 2 directly. We get 2 squared minus 4 over 2 minus 2, which gives 0 over 0.

**[3:04 – 3:14] Amber warning box**
0 over 0 is called an indeterminate form. Careful—this does NOT mean the answer is zero! It just means we need to simplify the expression first.

**[3:14 – 3:25] Step 2: Factoring the top**
Step 2: Factor the top part. Since x squared minus 4 is a difference of squares, it splits into (x minus 2) times (x plus 2).

**[3:25 – 3:34] Step 3: Rewrite fraction**
Step 3: Put that back into the fraction. Look closely—we now have (x minus 2) on both the top and the bottom.

**[3:34 – 3:51] Step 4: Red slashes cancel (x - 2)**
Step 4: Cancel out (x minus 2). Why can we do that? Because in a limit, x only gets close to 2—it is never equal to 2. So (x minus 2) isn't zero, and canceling it is completely fine. That simplifies our function to x plus 2, as long as x is not equal to 2.

**[3:51 – 4:03] Step 5: Final green box**
Step 5: Now just plug x equals 2 into the simplified part: x plus 2 becomes 2 plus 2, which equals 4. That matches our graph perfectly!

**[4:03 – 4:10] Graph on left side**

**[4:10 – 4:17] Left-hand limit card + blue dot**

**[4:17 – 4:25] Right-hand limit card + purple dot**
When x approaches 2 from the right—shown with a little plus sign—the right-hand limit of f of x is also 4.

**[4:25 – 4:32] Two-sided limit card**
Since the left-hand limit and the right-hand limit are both 4, the overall limit equals 4.

**[4:32 – 4:48] Bottom comparison cards**
And notice the big difference here: the limit at x equals 2 is 4, even though the function value f of 2 is undefined. The limit exists because both sides head to the same number, even though there's an empty hole right at x equals 2.
